## Start with the business, not the chart

Over a one to five year horizon the chart is a symptom. What compounds is return on the
capital the business employs, reinvested at a rate the company can sustain. The desk's
quality checklist for MONTH and YEAR ideas:

- **Return on equity and on invested capital**: double-digit ROE sustained across a full
  cycle, and ROIC comfortably above the cost of capital (roughly 10-12% in a Philippine
  rate environment).
- **Margin stability**: gross margin that does not swing with every input cost print.
  Pricing power shows up as stable margins through an inflation cycle.
- **Cash conversion**: operating cash flow tracking net income over several years. A
  company that reports profit but never generates cash is financing its own receivables.
- **Balance sheet**: net debt to EBITDA under about 3x for industrials, interest cover
  above 4x, and no reliance on short-term peso borrowings to fund long assets.
- **Management behaviour**: consistent disclosure, sane capital allocation, buybacks or
  dividends funded by free cash flow rather than by new debt.

These are slow-moving variables, which is exactly why they support a slow-moving
position.

## Valuation: pay a fair price for a good business

Cheap is not the same as good value, and a low multiple on deteriorating earnings is a
value trap. The desk anchors on a small set of comparable measures:

- **P/E versus the name's own history and its sector**: a bank at 8x through the cycle is
  normal; a consumer staple at 8x usually means the market doubts the earnings.
- **PEG near or below 1**: earnings growth you can underwrite, priced at or under that
  growth rate. PEG above 2 asks the business to beat expectations for years.
- **Dividend yield with a coverage test**: payout ratio below roughly 70% of free cash
  flow. A 7% yield with a 120% payout ratio is a pending dividend cut.
- **Price-to-book for financials**: meaningful for banks and property, misleading for
  asset-light services.
- **Enterprise value over EBITDA for capital-intensive names**: utilities, telcos,
  cement, power generation — where depreciation is real and leverage is structural.

A quality business bought at a demanding multiple still delivers a poor five-year
return. A mediocre business bought cheaply delivers a poor decade. The desk wants the
intersection, and it is content to own fewer names.

## Position building for long horizons

- Enter in tranches: an initial position on valuation support, additions on confirmation
  from the next quarterly report. Long horizons do not require a perfect entry, but they
  do require a reason to add.
- Stops for MONTH and YEAR ideas are thesis stops, not price stops. A close below the
  200-day average is a warning; a broken thesis — lost franchise, regulatory reversal,
  permanently impaired balance sheet — is the exit.
- Volatility is the price of the return. Size positions so a 30% drawdown in one name is
  uncomfortable but not decisive.
- Reinvest dividends rather than treating them as income if the goal is compounding.

## Sector lenses that matter on the PSE

- **Banks**: net interest margin, loan growth, non-performing loan ratio, cost-to-income,
  capital adequacy. Rate cuts compress margins with a lag but support loan demand and
  credit quality.
- **Property**: pre-sales, inventory mix, unrecognised revenue backlog, net gearing,
  interest cost capitalisation. Residential oversupply shows up in margins before it
  shows up in sales.
- **Utilities and power**: regulated versus merchant exposure, fuel pass-through,
  capacity additions, WESM price sensitivity.
- **Consumer and retail**: same-store sales growth, gross margin, store rollout economics,
  input cost pass-through.
- **Holding companies**: sum-of-the-parts discount to net asset value, and whether
  management is closing or widening it. A persistent 40% discount is the market's price
  for governance, not a free option.
- **Mining and commodities**: cost curve position, grade, permitting, commodity price
  exposure. Earnings here are cyclical, so a "cheap" P/E at the top of the cycle is the
  most expensive multiple you can pay.

## Red flags that end a long-horizon thesis

- Recurring "one-off" charges, or earnings that only look healthy before impairments.
- Related-party transactions that move cash to an affiliate at non-market terms.
- Auditors resigning, qualified opinions, or repeated delays in filing reports.
- Serial equity issuance: each SRO or placement resets the per-share compounding story.
- Dividend funded by debt while management talks about growth capex.
- A controlling shareholder whose interests diverge from the minority's on every capital
  decision.

Over a year, one governance event can erase several years of business progress. That
asymmetry is why the desk hard-disqualifies suspension risk and structural dilution at
every horizon, including the long ones.
