## The three macro dials that move the PSEi

Emerging-market equity returns are driven locally by three variables, and on the PSE they
arrive in a predictable order: policy rates, the peso, and foreign fund flows.

- **BSP policy rate.** The Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas sets the overnight reverse
  repurchase rate. Cuts are a tailwind for banks (with a lag, through loan growth and
  credit quality), property (mortgage affordability), utilities (cheaper funding) and
  consumer names (discretionary demand). Hikes do the opposite, and banks often trade
  better early in a hiking cycle on margin expansion.
- **Inflation and the peso.** A weak peso imports inflation through fuel and food, which
  squeezes consumer margins and pushes the central bank to stay tight. Exporters and
  dollar earners — mining, some industrials, BPO-exposed names — benefit at the margin.
- **Foreign flows.** The PSE is a small, liquid-at-the-top market: daily net foreign
  buying or selling in the index heavyweights often explains the index move better than
  any domestic news. Sustained net selling caps rallies regardless of the local story.

Read the direction of these dials before allocating a MONTH or YEAR position. The same
quality name compounds faster with the policy wind behind it.

## Sector map and rate sensitivity

The PSEi is concentrated, and each cluster has a distinct macro sensitivity:

- **Banks and financials** — the largest index weight. Sensitive to the policy path,
  credit growth and provisioning. Rate cuts help credit quality and loan demand but
  compress margins; the net effect usually turns positive after a couple of quarters.
- **Property** — levered to mortgage rates, remittance-funded demand and pre-sale
  velocity. Early-cycle beneficiary of easing, but oversupply can override the rate
  tailwind for years.
- **Utilities, power and telcos** — bond proxies. Dividend yields compete with government
  debt; falling rates make their yields relatively more attractive and cheapen their
  project financing.
- **Consumer staples and retail** — inflation is the swing factor. Input cost pass-through
  and same-store sales growth separate the winners.
- **Holding companies** — conglomerates that own the above, usually at a persistent
  discount to net asset value. They are a cheap way to own a diversified macro view, and
  a poor way to express a single-sector conviction.
- **Mining and energy** — commodity price takers. Their earnings are a function of global
  prices and the peso, not domestic demand.

Rotate toward the clusters whose macro dial is improving, and avoid owning three
clusters that all depend on the same dial turning.

## Remittances, BPO and the domestic demand engine

Two structural dollar inflows underpin Philippine consumption:

- **OFW remittances** arrive monthly, peak in December, and flow disproportionately into
  food, housing, education and small business investment. They support consumer staples,
  retail and mass-market property.
- **BPO and IT-enabled services** pay local wages in dollars, adding resilient
  middle-class demand in Metro Manila, Cebu and Clark, and supporting office property
  absorption.

Both are relatively insensitive to domestic policy and highly sensitive to global
demand, US visa or immigration policy and the peso. When global growth slows, these
channels soften with a lag — which shows up in consumer names before it shows up in the
index.

## Flows, index events and the foreign ownership cap

- **Index rebalancing** (PSEi and sector indices) forces passive buying and selling on
  known dates. Additions can rally into the effective date and sell off afterwards;
  deletions do the reverse. Do not confuse the flow with a fundamental re-rating.
- **MSCI and FTSE reviews** create large one-day passive flows in the large caps. The
  signal is usually the announcement, not the effective date.
- **Foreign ownership limits**: banks and utilities carry a 40% foreign ownership cap,
  and media is closed to foreign equity entirely. When the cap binds, the premium between
  local and foreign boards is a real constraint on who can own the stock.
- **Free float matters.** A name with a 20% float can double on modest flow and halve just
  as fast. Position size for the liquidity, not for the story.

## Reading domestic policy risk

- **Franchise, tariff and rate cases** (telcos, water, power distribution) reset earnings
  for years. A pending rate case is a reason to wait for the decision, not to anticipate
  it.
- **Tax and regulatory changes** — excise, mining fiscal regimes, gaming — change the
  cash flow base directly.
- **Infrastructure spending** lifts cement, construction and select industrials, but the
  earnings arrive on project awards, not on budget announcements.
- **Elections** create a spending impulse and then a fiscal hangover. Treat pre-election
  enthusiasm as a flow event with a known expiry date.

For MONTH and YEAR ideas, the desk prefers a business whose earnings do not depend on a
single unresolved policy decision, and a macro dial that is turning in its favour. That
combination is rarer than a chart pattern, and it is worth waiting for.
